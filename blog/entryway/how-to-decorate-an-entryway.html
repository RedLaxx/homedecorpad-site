How to Decorate an Entryway: The Complete Guide to a Welcoming, Organized Entry

Every trip in or out of your home goes through the entryway — and it's the part of the house people decorate last. This complete guide covers why the entryway matters, what every entry needs, how to plan one for your space and where to go next.

Every trip in or out of your home goes through the entryway. It's the first space a guest sees, the last one you pass on the way to work, and the spot where keys, shoes, bags and mail pile up. It's also the part of the house people decorate last, or never, because it's small and always in use.

That's a missed opportunity. A good entryway takes less money and fewer square feet than almost any other room, and it changes how the whole home feels and works. This guide explains why the entryway matters, what every entry needs, how to plan one for your space and where to go next. It's also the hub for our entryway series of 21 posts, so each section links to the detailed guide for that topic.

Why the entryway matters

The entryway sets the tone. Before a guest sees your living room, they see your entry. A tidy one with a mirror, a lamp and a place for coats says the house is looked after. A pile of shoes and unopened mail says something else, and that impression often carries into the rest of the house. You feel it too. Walking into a cluttered entry after a long day adds friction at the exact moment you want to relax.

It's also the busiest working space in the house. Everything that comes in has to land somewhere: coats, bags, shoes, umbrellas, keys, packages, dog leashes. If the entry has no home for those things, they spread to the sofa, the kitchen counter and the bottom of the stairs. A few well-placed hooks, a bench and a cabinet stop that spread before it starts.

Third, it costs little. Because the space is small, a mirror, a rug and a lamp do most of the work, and you can finish an entry for far less than a bedroom or a kitchen. Few rooms give you as much improvement for the money.

Finally, you use it every day. You'll pass through the entryway at least twice a day, so every small improvement, like a bowl for keys or a bench for putting on shoes, gets used constantly. A piece in a guest room might be seen a few times a month. A piece in the entryway is seen all the time.

What every entryway needs

Whatever size your entry is, it has to do five jobs.

  • A place to drop things. A console table, a shelf or a bowl near the door holds keys, mail and wallets.
  • A place to hang things. Hooks or a peg rail take coats and bags, at a height everyone in the house can reach.
  • A place to sit. A bench or a stool makes it easy to put on shoes, and in a tight entry a slim bench against the wall is enough.
  • A place for shoes. A closed cabinet, a rack or a few baskets keeps shoes off the floor.
  • Light and a mirror. Good light makes the space feel welcoming, and a mirror adds brightness and lets you check how you look on the way out.

Add a rug to catch dirt and define the space, plus one or two decorative touches such as art or a plant, and the entry is done. A very small entry can't hold everything on this list, so pick the two or three items that solve your biggest daily annoyance and start there.

Start with your type of entry

The right plan depends on the space you have.

If your front door opens straight into the living room, as it does in many apartments, you need to create an entry zone with a rug, a slim console, a mirror or a floating shelf with hooks. Our guide to apartment entryway ideas covers it.

If the entry is small, choose furniture that holds storage, hang things on the wall and use a large mirror. Start with small entryway ideas.

If the space is long and thin, keep furniture shallow and leave a clear walking path. See narrow entryway ideas.

If you have a large foyer, you can use a round table, a bench and a cabinet, but you'll need scale and good lighting so the room doesn't feel empty. Our main guide, entryway decor, is a good place to start.

If your entry is outside, on a porch or at the front door, see front door decor.

How to plan your entryway

Work through these steps in order, and the decisions get easier.

  1. Measure the space. Note the width, the depth and how far the front door swings, and keep a clear walking path about 36 inches (91 cm) wide wherever you can.
  1. List what lands there. Count the coats, bags, pairs of shoes and other things that come through the door every day, so you can size the storage.
  1. Choose the furniture. In a narrow space, most slim console tables run about 10 to 14 inches deep. A bench or cabinet needs more room, so check the swing of the door first.
  1. Hang a mirror. Choose one about two-thirds the width of the table below it, and center it near eye level, around 57 inches (145 cm) from the floor.
  1. Add light. A lamp on the table or a pair of sconces gives a warm glow, and a 2700K bulb keeps it flattering.
  1. Lay a rug. Pick one wide enough that the front door clears it and that you can still walk across safely, and use a non-slip pad underneath.
  1. Finish with one or two touches, such as a piece of art, a plant or a tray for keys.

Common entryway mistakes

  • Choosing furniture that's too deep. In a narrow entry, a bulky bench or table blocks the walkway and makes the space feel smaller.
  • Skipping storage. Without a home for shoes and bags, decor can't compete with clutter.
  • Hanging a mirror that's too small. A small mirror above a long console looks lost, so go wider.
  • Forgetting light. A dim entry feels unwelcoming no matter how good the furniture is.
  • Buying a rug that's too small or too thick. A small rug slides and trips people, and a very thick one can catch the front door.
  • Decorating without a plan. Measure first, so you don't buy pieces that won't fit.
What should an entryway have?

An entryway should have a place to drop things, a place to hang coats and bags, a place to sit, a place for shoes, and good light with a mirror. Add a rug and one or two decorative touches to finish it.

How do I decorate an entryway with no foyer?

Create a defined zone just inside the door. A rug, a slim console or floating shelf, a mirror and a few hooks give you a drop zone without taking much floor space. See our guide to apartment entryway ideas.

How do I make a small entryway look bigger?

Use a large mirror, keep the walls light, mount storage on the wall instead of the floor and choose slim furniture. See small entryway ideas for more.

What do you put on an entryway table?

A lamp, a tray or bowl for keys, a small plant and a piece of art or a mirror above it cover most needs. Our guide to entryway table decor has 15 ideas.
